\subsection{Learning in Cobweb and Fishery Models}
\label{section:fc_dual_sim}

Two simulations compare nine learning paradigms in the cobweb model and seven in the fishery model.
The first is a cobweb model with adjustment cost, a self-referential environment in which the agent's own action moves the contemporaneous price.
The second is a logistic-growth fishery in which harvest depletes a renewable stock without changing a contemporaneous price through expectations.
Six paradigms are common to both panels, an oracle that knows the true parameters, a precautionary constant-rule baseline that does not learn, recursive least squares adaptive learning \citep{MarcetSargent1989}, the genetic algorithm of \citet{Arifovic1994}, tabular Q-learning \citep{WatkinsDayan1992}, and a model-based learner that estimates the structural coefficients by least squares and plans through a discounted Bellman equation (closed-form linear-quadratic on the cobweb, grid-based dynamic programming on the fishery).
The cobweb panel adds three model-based policy-gradient learners that share a bootstrap-ensemble linear-Gaussian world model and a linear policy but differ in the gradient estimator.
Following the short-rollout design of \citet{janner2019model}, the first learner branches model rollouts from replay-buffer states and updates a Gaussian linear policy by REINFORCE.\footnote{We label this learner MB-LG-REINFORCE because it retains MBPO's short rollouts branched from observed states but changes the model class and policy optimizer. MBPO uses a bootstrap ensemble of probabilistic neural networks and SAC. The simulation instead uses bootstrap linear-Gaussian regressions and a two-parameter Gaussian policy, and feeds the rollouts directly into a score-function update. Results are therefore not a direct comparison with MBPO.}
The second is a variance-reduced variant of the same estimator that pairs antithetic rollouts with a state-dependent baseline.
The third is a pathwise variant that differentiates the model-rollout return analytically through a forward sensitivity recursion.
The fishery simulation instead adds a myopic open-access agent that maximizes current profit and ignores the stock transition. This represents the standard bioeconomic collapse benchmark.
Both environments have low-dimensional states, smooth payoffs, and fixed parametric forms. The results therefore apply to these structured settings and do not establish a general ranking.

\subsubsection{Cobweb with adjustment cost}
\label{section:fc_dual_sim_cobweb}

The cobweb experiment reports cumulative regret, parameter recovery, and policy distance for three values of $b/c$. In this single-agent monopoly model, changing $b/c$ changes the curvature of the value function. The experiment does not implement the self-referential perceived-law setting for which \citet{MarcetSargent1989} derive least-squares convergence conditions.

The state is $s_t = (q_{t-1}, p_{t-1}) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ and the action is $q_t \in [0, 4]$.
The price clears contemporaneously as $p_t = a - b q_t + \varepsilon_t$ with $\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, and the reward is $r_t = p_t q_t - (c/2) q_t^2 - (\phi/2)(q_t - q_{t-1})^2$, so the agent faces a linear demand, a quadratic production cost, and a quadratic adjustment cost.
The sweep is $b/c \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$ with $a = 4$, $c = 1$, $\phi = 0.2$, $\sigma = 0.1$, $\gamma = 0.95$, $T = 500$, and twenty independent seeds.
The oracle uses the affine feedback rule $q_t^\star = K_0 + K_q q_{t-1}$ obtained by fixed-point iteration on the quadratic Bellman equation. Cumulative regret is computed per seed against the oracle's realized return on the same noise sequence.

All nine methods receive the same 500-step environment budget in each seed.
The oracle knows $(a, b, c, \phi)$ and applies the closed-form optimal feedback rule.
The constant-rule baseline plays $q_t = 1.4$ at every step regardless of state and serves as a fixed no-learning comparator.
Recursive least squares of \citet{MarcetSargent1989} estimates $(\hat a, \hat b)$ from observed prices, treats $(c, \phi)$ as known, and re-solves the linear-quadratic planner with point estimates each period.\footnote{Recursive least squares is given $(c, \phi)$, whereas the model-based LQ and three policy-gradient learners estimate all four coefficients. Their regret differences therefore combine differences in prior information with differences in planning.}
The genetic algorithm of \citet{Arifovic1994} maintains a population of binary-encoded production rules, evolves them under fitness-proportional selection on the running mean of realized observed profit, single-point crossover, bit-flip mutation, and two-elite preservation, and plays the population's incumbent rule.
In Arifovic's augmented genetic algorithm, the election operator evaluates potential offspring at the previous period's market price using the known cost function before comparing them with their parents. The simulation omits this operator, so selection depends only on realized profit.
Tabular Q-learning of \citet{WatkinsDayan1992} discretizes the state-action space onto a $20 \times 20 \times 25$ grid and applies $\varepsilon$-greedy temporal-difference updates with $\alpha = 0.1$ and $\varepsilon$ decaying linearly from $0.3$ to $0.01$.
All four model-based learners estimate demand and cost coefficients from observed transitions and rewards. The LQ learner uses point estimates, while the three policy-gradient learners fit bootstrap ensembles for demand and least-squares estimates for cost.
The model-based LQ learner solves the linear-quadratic Bellman equation by Riccati iteration on current point estimates of $(\hat a, \hat b, \hat c, \hat \phi)$ at each step. It applies the resulting affine feedback rule with Gaussian exploration noise.
The MB-LG-REINFORCE learner adopts the short branched-rollout design of \citet{janner2019model}, maintains five bootstrap linear-Gaussian demand models, and parameterizes the policy as $q = K_0 + K_q q_{t-1}$.
At each real step it samples ten rollouts of horizon five from buffer-uniform initial states under random ensemble members, accumulates discounted returns from the learned reward model, and updates $(K_0, K_q)$ by REINFORCE with a moving-average baseline.
The MB-LG-VR learner keeps the REINFORCE learner's estimator, rollout budget, and learning rate but simulates its rollouts in antithetic pairs, replaying each pair's Gaussian draws with the sign flipped, and replaces the scalar moving-average baseline with a quadratic state-dependent baseline fitted by least squares on past rollout returns only.
In a diagnostic with two thousand draws at a fixed policy and fitted model, antithetic pairing reduced the estimated variance of the $K_0$ update direction by a factor of $19.10$ with the same number of rollouts.\footnote{The estimated mean $K_0$ update directions were $14.16$ under REINFORCE and $15.12$ under antithetic pairing.}
The MB-LG-Pathwise learner uses the same ensemble fitting procedure and linear policy class but replaces the score-function estimator with an analytic pathwise gradient through a forward sensitivity recursion. The implementation uses twenty horizon-five rollouts per real step and a learning rate of $0.05$, compared with ten rollouts and $0.005$ for the other two policy-gradient learners; gradients are clipped to unit $\ell_2$ norm.

Table~\ref{table:fc_cobweb_results} reports cumulative regret at $T = 500$ for each paradigm and regime; Figure~\ref{figure:fc_cobweb_curves} shows the corresponding regret trajectories. The table orders the paradigms by mean regret across the three regimes. Recursive least squares attains regret of about five units in every regime, because it has the correct functional form for the price map and known cost parameters and need only estimate the two demand coefficients. The model-based LQ learner records regret from about twelve units in the stable regime to forty-three units in the unstable regime and estimates the cost coefficients as well as the demand map. The MB-LG-VR learner records thirty to forty-four units of regret, while the MB-LG-Pathwise learner records thirty-five to forty-seven. MB-LG-VR uses the same rollout budget and learning rate as plain REINFORCE. The pathwise learner instead uses twenty rather than ten rollouts per update, a learning rate of $0.05$ rather than $0.005$, and gradient clipping. The modified Arifovic-style genetic algorithm without the election operator records regret from about ninety to three hundred units. It searches over binary-encoded quantities using realized-profit selection, crossover, mutation, and two-elite preservation, without estimating demand or cost parameters. The fixed-action rule, labeled Naive in the table, records cumulative regret of $179.73$ in the stable regime, $3.37$ in the near-unit-root regime, and $450.34$ in the unstable regime. MB-LG-REINFORCE regret varies by more than an order of magnitude across regimes, from about six hundred and fifty in the stable regime to forty-nine in the unstable regime. Because the experiment changes the regime rather than curvature alone, it does not identify the mechanism behind this difference. Tabular Q-learning records eight hundred to one thousand units of regret under the specified grid, exploration schedule, and 500-step interaction budget. Under the reported hyperparameters, branched-rollout REINFORCE has higher regret than closed-form planning in all three regimes. Stable-regime regret is 656.60 for MB-LG-REINFORCE, 47.07 for MB-LG-Pathwise, and 38.42 for MB-LG-VR. MB-LG-VR modifies the score-function estimate through antithetic pairs and a state-dependent baseline while retaining the plain REINFORCE rollout budget and learning rate. The pathwise comparison also changes the rollout budget, learning rate, and clipping rule, so it does not isolate the gradient estimator. The three MB-LG learners share the bootstrap-ensemble parameter estimator, while the model-based LQ learner uses point estimates and Riccati iteration.

Cumulative regret aggregates losses over the 500-step episode, while the recovery and policy-distance diagnostics measure the learners at the final step. Figure~\ref{figure:fc_cobweb_recovery} plots the parameter trajectories $\hat a_t$ and $\hat b_t$ for recursive least squares, the model-based LQ learner, and the MB-LG-REINFORCE and MB-LG-Pathwise ensemble means against the true values. Table~\ref{table:fc_cobweb_recovery} reports the final $|\hat\theta - \theta|$ error per paradigm and regime.\footnote{The MB-LG-VR learner runs the identical estimation step and recovers the coefficients comparably (demand coefficients within two percent, cost coefficients to machine precision); it is omitted from the recovery figure and table for legibility.} For all four methods, the final mean demand-coefficient estimates are within four percent of the truth in every regime. Across all four methods and all three regimes, the mean estimates enter and remain within four percent of the true values after at most 34 environment steps. The model-based learners additionally recover the cost coefficients $(c, \phi)$ to within machine precision\footnote{The exact recovery reflects noiseless reward in this simulation: given observed $(p_t, q_t, q_{t-1})$, the equation $r_t = p_t q_t - (c/2) q_t^2 - (\phi/2)(q_t - q_{t-1})^2$ is exactly solvable for $(c, \phi)$ from any two tuples that span the cost feature space. With additive reward noise, the regression would no longer recover the coefficients exactly.} because the reward signal pins them down once the residual $r_t - p_t q_t$ is regressed on $(q_t^2, (q_t - q_{t-1})^2)$. At the final step, recursive least squares has its smallest mean absolute errors for both demand coefficients in the unstable regime. The model-based LQ learner has its smallest mean absolute error for $a$ in the unstable regime and for $b$ in the borderline regime.

Figure~\ref{figure:fc_cobweb_policy_distance} reports the distance from each learner's noise-free action at a regime-specific reference state to the oracle's action at the same state, on a log scale. At $T = 500$, the model-based LQ learner has the smallest mean distance in every regime. Its distances are 0.031, 0.012, and 0.004, compared with 0.057, 0.027, and 0.010 for recursive least squares. MB-LG-VR and MB-LG-Pathwise remain close to recursive least squares, while the genetic algorithm has larger final distances of 0.177, 0.077, and 0.062. MB-LG-REINFORCE records 1.462 in the stable regime and 0.016 in the unstable regime. Tabular Q-learning records final distances between 0.610 and 1.117 under its $20 \times 20 \times 25$ discretization. The constant rule has distances of 0.600, 0.067, and 0.600 because its action is fixed at $q_{\text{fixed}} = 1.4$.

Averaged across the three regimes at $T = 500$, recursive least squares has the lowest mean regret among the non-oracle methods, while the model-based LQ learner has the smallest final policy distance in every regime. Recursive least squares is given the true cost parameters and estimates only the demand coefficients. The model-based LQ learner estimates both demand and cost coefficients before applying the closed-form planner. The MB-LG learners instead optimize a linear policy through model rollouts. These results apply to the specified linear-quadratic environment and do not establish an ordering for environments without a closed-form planner.

\subsubsection{Fishery with logistic growth}
\label{section:fc_dual_sim_fishery}

The fishery simulation compares seven policies under logistic stock growth. Harvest affects the next stock directly, but there is no expectation-dependent price. Because the stock transition is nonlinear, the oracle uses grid-based dynamic programming.

The state is the stock biomass $s_t \in [0, 1.5 K]$ and the action is the harvest $h_t \in [0, \min(s_t, h_{\max})]$. The dynamics are $s_{t+1} = \max(0, s_t + r s_t (1 - s_t / K) - h_t + \varepsilon_t)$ with $\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, and the reward is $r_t = p h_t - (c/2) h_t^2$. Parameters are $r = 0.4$, $K = 10$, $p = 2.0$, $c = 0.2$, $\sigma = 0.3$, $\gamma = 0.95$, $T = 500$, with twenty independent seeds and $s_0 = K$ (initial stock at carrying capacity). The deterministic maximum-sustainable-yield harvest is $h_{\text{MSY}} = r K / 4 = 1$ at $s^\star = K / 2 = 5$.

The seven policies are the oracle, a myopic policy, a constant-harvest baseline, recursive least squares, model-based dynamic programming, tabular Q-learning, and an adapted genetic algorithm.
The oracle solves a grid-based value-iteration discounted dynamic program on $50$ stock $\times$ $25$ action points and applies the greedy policy.
The myopic policy maximizes the per-period profit $p h_t - (c/2) h_t^2$ at each step and ignores the stock transition.
This delivers the unconstrained interior solution $h_t^{\textsc{my}} = p / c = 10$.
The environment clips realized harvest to $\min(s_t, h_{\max})$, where $h_{\max} = 1.5 \cdot rK/4 = 1.5$. The myopic policy therefore harvests fifty percent above the maximum sustainable yield while the stock remains above the cap.
The constant-rule baseline harvests $h = 0.5$ at every step. This value lies between zero and the deterministic maximum-sustainable-yield harvest.
Recursive least squares estimates $(\hat r, \hat K)$ from a linear-in-parameters regression of $\Delta s_t + h_t$ on $(s_t, -s_t^2)$, plans by re-solving the DP with point estimates every twenty-five observations, and applies the resulting greedy policy with known cost parameters $(p, c)$.
The model-based DP learner estimates $(\hat r, \hat K, \hat p, \hat c)$ by batch least squares, resolves the DP every twenty-five observations, and adds Gaussian exploration noise that decreases over the episode. Its planner uses grid-based dynamic programming.
Tabular Q-learning discretizes $(s, h)$ onto a $30 \times 21$ grid with $\alpha = 0.1$ and $\varepsilon$ decaying from $0.3$ to $0.01$.
The genetic algorithm adapts the fitness-proportional reproduction, crossover, mutation, and election operators in \citet{Arifovic1994} to a population of thirty binary-encoded constant-harvest rules. The election comparison uses the known values of $p$ and $c$.\footnote{\citet{Arifovic1994} evaluates proposed offspring at the previous market price and compares the two offspring with their two parents. This simulation instead compares each child only with its selected parent using one-period fishery profit at the most recently observed stock. The fishery implementation is therefore an adaptation of the election operator, not a reproduction of the source algorithm.}
Q-learning defines its action grid on $[0,h_{\max}]$, the genetic algorithm decodes its chromosomes to the same interval, and the model-based DP learner clips exploratory actions to that interval. Here, $h_{\max} = 1.5 \cdot r K / 4$ is computed from the true $r$ and $K$.\footnote{The simulation supplies this action bound. A model-free learner without prior knowledge of $r$ and $K$ would require an exogenous bound or a separate procedure for choosing the action range.}

Table~\ref{table:fc_fishery_results} reports cumulative regret at $T = 500$, and Figure~\ref{figure:fc_fishery_curves} plots the regret trajectories. Mean final regret is 13.67 for recursive least squares, 14.69 for model-based dynamic programming, 274.71 for Q-learning, 447.35 for the constant rule, 706.13 for the genetic algorithm, and 753.11 for the myopic policy. Under the myopic policy, the stock first reaches zero between steps 11 and 23 across the twenty seeds. At $T = 500$, the mean absolute errors in the recursive least-squares estimates are 0.007 for $r$ and 0.219 for $K$; the corresponding model-based DP errors are 0.006 and 0.180. The genetic algorithm searches only over constant-harvest rules, whereas the oracle policy varies harvest with the stock.
