\subsection{Synthesis}
\label{section:fc_synthesis}

The chapter begins with Sutton's Dyna and Schmidhuber's 1990 controller-model architecture. It then covers the V-M-C design of \citet{HaSchmidhuber2018}, recurrent state-space models in PlaNet and Dreamer, value-aware objectives and MuZero, short branched rollouts in MBPO, and latent model-predictive control in TD-MPC2. These methods differ in what the model predicts and how its predictions enter policy learning.

\subsubsection{Empirical advantages}
\label{section:fc_synthesis_wins}

Model-based methods can reduce environment interaction by using learned dynamics for planning or for policy and value learning. PlaNet and Dreamer apply learned latent dynamics to image-based continuous control, MuZero combines a learned latent model with tree search in board games and Atari, and MBPO uses short model rollouts for continuous control. TD-MPC2 trains a shared latent dynamics model across multiple tasks. These results are specific to the reported benchmarks and comparisons. They do not establish that model-based methods dominate model-free alternatives whenever interaction is limited.

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\label{section:fc_synthesis_fails}

One limitation is the mismatch between fitting a predictive model and obtaining a high-return controller. \citet{Lambert2020} report a correlation of $0.07$ between one-step log likelihood and controller reward for models evaluated on their Half-Cheetah expert dataset. In a separate cartpole example, they adjust the last layer of a learned dynamics model so that validation log likelihood rises from $4.827$ to $4.85$ while reward falls from $176$ to $98$ over five trials. \citet{VoelckerCalib2025} study a different issue. They show that sampled MuZero and IterVAML losses are uncalibrated surrogate losses for stochastic environment models and tend to learn models with too little variance. Their calibrated loss removes the model-learning variance term. For MuZero-style value-function updates, they identify a separate bias that this correction does not remove. In their continuous-control experiments, the effect of calibration varies across losses and task suites. Value-aware objectives change which model errors are penalized, but they do not eliminate model error.

A second limitation is multi-step model error. \citet{Asadi2018Lipschitz} analyze a continuous-state Markov reward process with one action, a state-only Lipschitz reward, and a transition model whose one-step error is bounded in Wasserstein distance. Their value-error bound depends on $\bar K=\min\{K_F,K_T\}$, the smaller of the learned and true transition Lipschitz constants, and requires $\gamma\bar K<1$. The bound becomes uninformative when this condition is not satisfied. It does not show that planning itself must diverge.

\citet{Talvitie2017} instead train the model on paired environment and model rollouts. The model receives its own possibly incorrect state and predicts the next state reached by the environment under the same action. The paper proves its tighter error guarantees under deterministic dynamics and blind rollout policies. It also reports that hallucinated training can be unstable because model parameters affect the training distribution. \citet{Pathak2017} use forward prediction error in an inverse-dynamics feature space as intrinsic reward. \citet{Sekar2020} plan in a recurrent latent model to maximize disagreement among one-step predictors, then reuse the learned model for zero-shot or few-shot task adaptation. These exploration methods change data collection rather than the predictive objective itself.

\subsubsection{When some dynamics need not be learned}
\label{section:fc_synthesis_boundary}

Some environments do not require every transition mechanism to be learned from scratch. \citet{Madeka2022DeepInventory} treat demand, vendor lead times, prices, and costs as exogenous processes whose evolution is unaffected by ordering actions. This permits corrected historical trajectories to supply those variables to a simulator. Inventory and reward still depend on the actions, and DirectBackprop optimizes the policy through a differentiable inventory simulator. The paper's fully observed analysis bounds the error of estimating policy values from historical product trajectories. Its partially observed analysis adds a supervised imputation error term for censored exogenous variables. Neither result reduces the ordering problem to one-shot supervised prediction. The example therefore separates exogenous processes that can be replayed from the controlled inventory dynamics represented in the simulator.

\subsubsection{Open questions}
\label{section:fc_synthesis_open}

The simulations in \S\ref{section:fc_dual_sim} compare learning paradigms in the cobweb and fishery environments. They describe the sample requirements of the methods in these two settings and do not establish a general ranking. The cobweb methods also receive different structural information. Recursive least squares is given the correct demand regression form and the true cost parameters, the model-based learner estimates demand and cost coefficients, the population search uses binary-encoded production rules evaluated with realized profits, and Q-learning uses a tabular state-action representation. Their regret differences therefore combine the learning method with prior information, policy representation, and planner.

The cobweb experiment does not test the instability studied by \citet{MarcetSargent1989}, because the single producer's current quantity determines the contemporaneous price and no belief about a future price enters the decision. An expectational version would be needed to study whether a neural world model changes those learning dynamics. The cobweb planners also differ. The linear-quadratic planner uses a Riccati solution, whereas the branched-rollout REINFORCE planner estimates a gradient from simulated trajectories. The latter introduces sampling variance that is absent from the Riccati solution. Whether this difference persists when no analytical planner is available remains an empirical question.

The comparison with least-squares learning is limited. \citet{MarcetSargent1989} analyze recursive least-squares beliefs in self-referential linear stochastic models. Under their assumptions, local convergence to a fixed point is tied to stability of the differential equation $\dot{\beta}=T(\beta)-\beta$, while their sufficient conditions for global convergence use a larger differential equation that also tracks the regressor second-moment matrix. \citet{Asadi2018Lipschitz} instead bound rollout and value error for an approximate transition model under the assumptions stated above. The objects and convergence claims are different, so neither result provides a stability theory for joint model and policy learning in the economic environments considered here.

Across these examples, policy quality depends on how the model is trained, where it is accurate, and how the planner uses it. For economic counterfactuals, predictive fit alone does not establish that the induced policy or value estimate is accurate.
