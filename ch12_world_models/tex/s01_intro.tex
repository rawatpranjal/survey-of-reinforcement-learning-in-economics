\subsection{Learning Without Rational Expectations}
\label{section:fc_paradigms}

World models in reinforcement learning are learned forecasts of state transitions and rewards that agents use for planning. Economists have studied closely related learning problems since at least the 1980s. In this literature, an agent estimates the environment's law of motion from data and acts on that estimate rather than knowing the law of motion in advance. The agent's model at date $t$ is a parameter $\theta_t \in \Theta$ that indexes a transition estimate $\widehat{P}_{\theta_t}(s' \mid s, a)$ and a reward estimate $\widehat{r}_{\theta_t}(s, a)$. A planning operator $G$ returns a policy $\pi_t = G(\theta_t)$ that is optimal, or at least improved, under the current model. The agent draws a real transition $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$ with $a_t \sim \pi_t(\cdot \mid s_t)$. A learning operator $F$ then revises the model,
\[
  \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t + \alpha_t \, F(\theta_t, s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1}),
\]
where $\alpha_t$ is a gain sequence. Stochastic approximation studies exactly this kind of recursion. A rest point is a $\theta^\star$ at which the model is consistent with the data its own policy $G(\theta^\star)$ generates. Success hinges on two questions. The first is whether $\theta_t$ converges to a rest point. The second is whether the policy $G(\theta^\star)$ there is optimal on the true environment, which it need not be when the model class cannot contain the truth. The recursive least-squares example concerns convergence when the model can contain the truth. The Berk-Nash example concerns limiting beliefs and behavior when the subjective model may exclude the truth. Model-based reinforcement learning uses the same cycle of estimation, planning, action, and revision.

\subsubsection{Two economic examples of model-based learning}
\label{section:fc_paradigm_family}

The first example is recursive least squares adaptive learning \citep{MarcetSargent1989, EvansHonkapohja2001}. The agent holds a perceived law of motion, a parametric forecast of the variables it cares about, and updates its coefficients $\theta$ from realized data. With forecast target $p_t$, regressor $z_{t-1}$, and perceived disturbance $\varepsilon_t$, the perceived law of motion is $p_t = \theta' z_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ and the update is recursive least squares,
\[
  \theta_t = \theta_{t-1} + t^{-1} R_t^{-1} z_{t-1}\big(p_t - \theta_{t-1}' z_{t-1}\big), \qquad
  R_t = R_{t-1} + t^{-1}\big(z_{t-1} z_{t-1}' - R_{t-1}\big),
\]
with $R_t$ the running second-moment matrix of the regressor and $t^{-1}$ the decreasing gain. Decisions taken under the current belief feed back into the data, so a believed $\theta$ induces an actual law of motion whose coefficients form a map $T(\theta)$. A rational-expectations equilibrium is a fixed point $\bar\theta = T(\bar\theta)$, and convergence to it is governed by expectational stability, the local stability of $d\theta/d\tau = T(\theta) - \theta$ at $\bar\theta$, equivalently that the eigenvalues of $DT(\bar\theta) - I$ have strictly negative real parts. In a market with a single feedback coefficient $\lambda$ from average expectations to the realized outcome this reduces to $\lambda < 1$. Here the model can contain the truth, so the remaining issue is whether least-squares learning converges. Under the regularity conditions for the scalar model, beliefs converge almost surely when $\lambda < 1$; when $\lambda > 1$, convergence to the rational-expectations fixed point has probability zero.

The second example is misspecified Bayesian learning, formalized as Berk-Nash equilibrium \citep{EspondaPouzo2016}, where the model state is a belief $\mu_t \in \Delta(\Theta)$ rather than the point estimate $\theta_t$ of the loop above. The agent carries a subjective class of models $\{Q_\theta : \theta \in \Theta\}$ of the map from action $a$ to consequence $Y$, with payoff $u(a, Y)$ and reward $r = u(a, Y)$, and forms the predictive $\bar{Q}_\mu(\cdot \mid s, a) = \int_\Theta Q_\theta(\cdot \mid s, a)\, \mu(d\theta)$. Under repeated interaction the belief concentrates on the parameters minimizing a weighted Kullback-Leibler divergence to the true distribution $Q_\pi$ generated under the agent's own policy $\pi$,
\[
  K(\pi, \theta) = \sum_{s, a} \pi(a \mid s)\, \rho(s)\; \mathbb{E}_{Q_\pi(\cdot \mid s, a)}\!\left[\ln \frac{Q_\pi(Y \mid s, a)}{Q_\theta(Y \mid s, a)}\right], \qquad
  \Theta(\pi) = \arg\min_{\theta \in \Theta} K(\pi, \theta),
\]
where $\rho(s)$ is the distribution of the signal $s$. A Berk-Nash equilibrium is a pair $(\pi, \mu)$ with the policy optimal under the predictive belief, so that $\pi(a \mid s) > 0$ implies $a \in \arg\max_{a'} \mathbb{E}_{\bar{Q}_\mu(\cdot \mid s, a')}[u(a', Y)]$, and the belief supported on the best-fit set, $\operatorname{supp}(\mu) \subseteq \Theta(\pi)$. The weighting by the agent's own policy matters because actions chosen with zero probability receive zero weight. Conditional on intended behavior stabilizing, posterior mass outside every open neighborhood of the weighted-divergence minimizers vanishes. In the paper's perturbed game, any stable strategy profile under myopically optimal policies is Berk-Nash. Because the subjective model may exclude the truth and data do not restrict beliefs about untried actions, beliefs supporting a stable strategy can remain misspecified, and the stable strategy need not be optimal in the true environment. The same failure can arise in world-model learning when a misspecified model guides data collection (\S\ref{section:fc_synthesis_fails}). The well-specified counterpart in reinforcement learning is posterior sampling, which draws a model from the posterior and plans against it.

Fictitious play is the same loop in the multi-agent case, the estimated model being the empirical distribution of opponents' actions and the planner a best response \citep{Brown1951, FudenbergKreps1993, HofbauerSandholm2002, FudenbergLevine1998, Young2004}.

\label{section:fc_convergence_question}%
Not every economic learning scheme estimates a model of the environment. Genetic-algorithm learning \citep{Arifovic1994, Arifovic1995} evolves a population of decision rules according to realized fitness. Evolutionary heuristic switching \citep{BrockHommes1997} updates the weights on a fixed set of forecasting rules through a logit function of past performance. The genetic algorithm adapts decision rules directly. The Brock-Hommes switching mechanism changes weights over forecasting rules rather than fitting a transition or reward model for planning. Section~\ref{section:fc_dual_sim} compares an Arifovic-style genetic algorithm with recursive least squares and model-based reinforcement-learning methods in two fixed economic environments.

\subsection{Dyna and Differentiable World Models}
\label{section:fc_origins1990}

In 1990, \citet{sutton1990} and \citet{Schmidhuber1990} described two reinforcement-learning architectures that learn a model from interaction data and use it to update the policy. Dyna combines reinforcement learning from observed transitions with planning updates from a learned action model. Schmidhuber trains a recurrent model of environmental dynamics and backpropagates through it to update the controller. The papers use different terminology, and neither cites the other.

\subsubsection{Sutton's integrated architecture}
\label{section:fc_origins_sutton}

\citet{sutton1990} proposes Dyna as an architecture that interleaves three processes at every real time step. The agent performs a direct Q-learning update from the observed transition $(s, a, r, s')$, then updates a learned model $\widehat{P}(s' \mid s, a)$ and $\widehat{r}(s, a)$ from the same transition, then performs $K$ planning updates by sampling previously visited state-action pairs $(\tilde{s}, \tilde{a})$, querying the model for simulated outcomes $(\tilde{r}, \tilde{s}')$, and applying the same Q-update to those synthetic transitions. The ratio $K$ of planning steps to real steps controls how heavily the agent exploits its learned model between actions. When the learned model is accurate and planning updates are inexpensive relative to real interaction, model-generated updates can accelerate policy improvement. The MBPO short-rollout analysis in \citet{janner2019model} is in the same family.

Sutton explicitly allows the learned action model to be probabilistic and imperfect, and treats planning as useful when its cost is low relative to real interaction. Dyna leaves the choice of model representation, supervised fitting method, search-control procedure, and reinforcement-learning method largely open. Later methods use tabular counts, Gaussian processes, deep ensembles, recurrent state-space models, and implicit latent dynamics within the same general model-and-planner structure (\S\ref{section:fc_mbpo}; \S\ref{section:fc_tdmpc2}).

\subsubsection{Schmidhuber's controller-model architecture}
\label{section:fc_origins_schmidhuber}

\citet{Schmidhuber1990} proposes a paired architecture consisting of a recurrent model network $M$ and a controller network $C$. The model $M$ receives the controller's outputs together with current observations and is trained, self-supervised, to predict the next inputs to $C$, including sensory observations and reinforcement signals. The controller $C$ produces actions. In the parallel version $C$ and $M$ train concurrently, while in the sequential version $M$ is trained and frozen before $C$ is optimized. Once $M$ predicts well, the environment becomes differentiable from the controller's perspective, since the Jacobian of $M$ stands in for the unknown Jacobian of the true dynamics. Planning is then implemented as gradient descent through the model network, by unrolling $C$ and $M$ together for a fixed horizon and descending on a predicted pain or cost signal, without executing any real actions during the planning phase. The controller is therefore updated by analytic gradients backpropagated through the learned recurrent dynamics model.

In the notation of \citet{Schmidhuber1990}, let $y_i(t)$ be the activation of controller input $i$, including sensory and reinforcement inputs, and let $y_i^{\mathrm{pred}}(t)$ be the model's prediction. The model minimizes
\[
  \mathcal{L}_M(\theta)
  =
  \sum_t \sum_i
  \left(y_i(t)-y_i^{\mathrm{pred}}(t)\right)^2.
\]
If $\mathcal{P}$ indexes the pain and pleasure inputs and $c_i$ is the desired activation of input $i$, the controller minimizes
\[
  \mathcal{L}_C(\phi)
  =
  \sum_t \sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}}
  \left(c_i-r_i(t)\right)^2.
\]
For planning, the model weights are held fixed while the controller and model are unrolled for $H$ steps. The controller update is computed by backpropagating predicted reinforcement through the unrolled model.

Schmidhuber's curiosity mechanism derives an additional reinforcement signal from the mismatch between the model's predictions and subsequent observations. It encourages actions that return the controller to situations in which the model can still improve. \citet{Pathak2017} later uses forward-model prediction error in an inverse-dynamics feature space as intrinsic reward. \citet{HaSchmidhuber2018} implements a deep version of the controller-model architecture.

The methods use different model classes and planners. The Dyna-Q instance developed here stores the most recent outcome for each visited state-action pair and applies $K$ tabular $Q$-updates after each real step. Schmidhuber uses a differentiable recurrent model and updates the controller by backpropagating through the unrolled $M_\theta$. Both learn the model from real data, use it to improve the policy, and then act in the environment.
