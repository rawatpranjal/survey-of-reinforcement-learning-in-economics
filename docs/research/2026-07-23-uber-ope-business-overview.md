# Off-Policy Evaluation as Causal Inference for Sequential Decisions

_Date: July 23, 2026_

This memo is for `ch10b_rl_for_ci`. It is about off-policy evaluation, not offline reinforcement learning and not the later applications chapter. That later chapter can point back to this as the foundations layer: what is being estimated, when it is identified, which estimators exist, and how to stress-test their assumptions.

The core idea is simple. A system has historical logs generated by one policy. We want to evaluate another policy without running it. In reinforcement-learning language, the logged policy is the behavior policy and the policy being evaluated is the evaluation policy. In causal-inference language, this is a counterfactual estimand: what outcome would have been observed if the decision rule had been different?

That translation is the point of the chapter. OPE is not just an RL engineering trick. It is causal inference with sequential treatments, delayed outcomes, and state distributions that change when the policy changes.

The chapter should read in this order:

1. Define the counterfactual policy value.
2. State the assumptions that identify it.
3. Use dynamic treatment regimes to make the sequential causal object familiar.
4. Show support and overlap before showing value estimates.
5. Explain why horizon and state-distribution shift change the estimator problem.
6. Organize estimators by their nuisance objects.
7. Treat uncertainty, robustness, and sensitivity as part of the estimand, not as decoration after the estimate.

## 1. What OPE Estimates

The object of interest is the value of a fixed policy:

```text
J(pi_e) = E_{pi_e}[sum_{t=1}^H gamma^{t-1} R_t]
```

or, in an infinite-horizon discounted setting,

```text
J(pi_e) = E_{pi_e}[sum_{t>=0} gamma^t R_t].
```

Here `pi_e` is the evaluation policy, `R_t` is the reward at time `t`, and `gamma` discounts later rewards. The expectation is over trajectories that would unfold if the evaluation policy controlled the actions. The data, however, were generated by a behavior policy `pi_b`.

The Uehara, Shi, and Kallus review gives the canonical framing: OPE estimates the mean reward of an evaluation policy using historical data generated by a potentially different behavior policy, especially where experimentation is expensive, risky, or unavailable.[^uehara-definition] That is exactly why OPE belongs in a causal-inference chapter. The target is not a prediction of the observed data. It is a counterfactual policy value.

The same setup contains several familiar special cases.

- Contextual bandits are the one-step case. We observe state, action, and reward once, and ask what reward a different action rule would have produced.
- Finite-horizon RL is the sequential case. Actions affect future states, and the value is a sum of rewards along the trajectory.
- Infinite-horizon or average-reward RL is the steady-state case. The policy may induce a long-run state-action distribution, and OPE estimates value under that distribution.
- Dynamic treatment regimes are the statistics analogue. A policy maps patient history or state to treatment over time.

Offline RL is different. Offline RL tries to learn or optimize a new policy from a static dataset. OPE evaluates a specified policy from logs. Offline RL may use OPE, and OPE may support offline policy optimization, but the OPE estimand is simpler and more sharply causal: given this policy, what is its value?

The first discipline is therefore to separate the estimand from the estimator. The estimand is the counterfactual value of a target rule. Direct methods, importance sampling, doubly robust estimators, fitted Q-evaluation, marginal density ratios, and robust bounds are possible routes to that target. They are not the target itself.

## 2. Identification Comes Before Estimation

OPE starts with identification, not with an estimator. The main assumptions are the sequential versions of standard causal assumptions.

First is support or positivity. The evaluation policy must not choose actions that the behavior policy never chose in the same relevant state. In the Uehara review's non-dynamic formulation, weak positivity requires the support of the evaluation policy to be included in the support of the behavior policy for each state.[^uehara-assumptions] Sequentially, this requirement compounds over time. A target policy can have support at every single action and still induce future states that are nearly absent from the logs.

Second is consistency. The observed reward must be the reward for the action actually taken. In a sequential system, consistency also asks whether the logged state and reward correspond to the intervention being evaluated. If there are hidden versions of treatment, unrecorded overrides, or changing action definitions, the policy in the log is not the policy in the estimand.

Third is no unmeasured confounding, often called sequential ignorability. Conditional on the logged state or history, the behavior policy's action must be as-good-as-random with respect to the potential future outcomes. This is automatic under known randomized logging. It is not automatic in observational logs. If a human operator, hidden risk score, capacity constraint, fraud flag, or external shock affected both the action and future reward but is absent from the state, the OPE value is not identified without further assumptions.

Fourth is the structural model for time. Uehara, Shi, and Kallus distinguish non-Markov decision processes (NMDPs), time-varying MDPs (TMDPs), and time-homogeneous MDPs.[^uehara-nmdp-tmdp] In an NMDP, the relevant state may be the full history. In a TMDP, the current state is enough, but transitions can depend on time. In an MDP, transitions are Markov and time-homogeneous. These distinctions are not cosmetic. They decide which density ratios appear, which efficiency bound applies, and whether the horizon curse can be avoided.

The chapter should make this ordering explicit:

> An OPE estimate can be statistically precise and causally unidentified at the same time.

The model can return a number. The question is whether the number answers the causal question.

### The Causal Dictionary

One reason OPE is a useful bridge topic is that the same mathematical object wears different names across fields. The behavior policy is the propensity score or treatment-assignment mechanism. The evaluation policy is a dynamic treatment regime or intervention rule. A trajectory is a longitudinal record. A reward is an outcome, often with delayed consequences. The Q-function is an outcome regression for future cumulative reward. The density ratio is a reweighting function that maps the logged distribution to the counterfactual distribution induced by the target rule.

This dictionary is not just pedagogical. It prevents two common mistakes.

The first mistake is to treat propensities as an implementation detail. In causal language, a missing propensity is a missing assignment mechanism. If the action was randomized but the randomization probability was not logged, the information exists conceptually but not empirically. If the action was chosen by an unknown production rule, then the propensity must be estimated, and that estimation error is now part of the OPE problem.

The second mistake is to treat a Q-function as an ordinary forecasting model. A Q-function is trained on observed data, but it is used under a counterfactual policy distribution. A model that predicts the next reward well under the logging policy can still be a bad OPE nuisance function if it is wrong in the regions emphasized by the evaluation policy. This is why doubly robust and targeted estimators care about how nuisance errors enter the final value estimate, not just about generic predictive accuracy.

The same point applies to density ratios. A ratio model is not useful because it classifies logged and target data in the abstract. It is useful because it reweights the parts of the logged process needed for the target policy value. In long-horizon problems, the relevant ratio may be a full-history ratio, a time-indexed state-action ratio, or a stationary occupancy ratio, depending on the assumed data-generating process.

The chapter should therefore present OPE as an estimand-first workflow:

1. Define the counterfactual policy value.
2. State the causal and time-series assumptions that identify it.
3. Check support and overlap for the evaluation policy.
4. Choose nuisance functions that match those assumptions.
5. Use an estimator whose error behavior is understood for those nuisance functions.
6. Report sensitivity when the assumptions are doubtful.

This order keeps the chapter from becoming a catalog of algorithms. The algorithms matter because each one encodes a particular answer to the same causal question.

### Dynamic Treatment Regimes Are Sequential Policies

Dynamic treatment regimes are the cleanest bridge between the causal-inference literature and off-policy evaluation. The DTR literature starts from personalized medicine, but the mathematical object is general: a rule maps an evolving history to a treatment. In the language of OPE, that rule is a policy.

The DTR tutorial by Wang and Tom defines a dynamic treatment regime as a sequence of treatment decision rules tailored to an individual's evolving status over time.[^wang-dtr-definition] In their formal setup, a regime `d = (d_1, ..., d_K)` maps covariate and treatment history to the next treatment, and the optimal regime maximizes the expected potential outcome `E[Y*(d)]`.[^wang-dtr-value] Laber, Lizotte, Qian, Pelham, and Murphy use the same idea: a DTR is a sequence of decision rules, one per stage, each mapping current patient information to a recommended treatment.[^laber-dtr-definition]

That is OPE vocabulary with different labels. The behavior policy is the treatment mechanism that generated the observed longitudinal data. The evaluation policy is the DTR whose value we want. The state is the available history. The action is the treatment. The reward is the outcome or cumulative outcome. The estimand is the expected counterfactual outcome under the target rule.

Dudik, Erhan, Langford, and Li make this dictionary explicit for contextual bandits: patient characteristics are contexts, treatments are actions, and a DTR is a policy.[^dudik-dtr-crosswalk] They also point out the important difference. In a simple contextual bandit, contexts are drawn independently and do not depend on previous actions. In a DTR, treatment can affect the patient's future state, so the longitudinal setting is more general.[^dudik-dtr-state]

This is why DTRs belong in a chapter on OPE foundations rather than in a medical side note. Estimating the mean response of a fixed DTR from observational longitudinal data is policy evaluation. Learning the best DTR is policy optimization. The distinction mirrors the one in RL: first define and identify the value of a fixed policy, then worry about searching over policies.

The identification assumptions are also the same assumptions in causal dress. Wang and Tom state consistency, positivity, and sequential randomization as the assumptions that connect DTR potential outcomes to observed data.[^wang-dtr-assumptions] Sonabend-W, Laha, Ananthakrishnan, Cai, and Mukherjee state the same triad for a two-stage DTR value problem: consistency, sequential ignorability, and positivity.[^sonabend-dtr-assumptions] If those assumptions fail, a DTR value estimate has the same problem as any other OPE estimate: it may be a precise number for the wrong counterfactual.

Sonabend-W et al. are the strongest direct source for the DTR/OPE link in this batch. Their paper proposes semi-supervised Q-learning and doubly robust off-policy value estimation for DTRs, and defines the value function as the expected counterfactual outcome under the derived regime.[^sonabend-ope-dtr] In their appendix, the general finite-horizon value estimator is explicitly a doubly robust off-policy value-function estimator, with the same horizon problem that appears in RL: products of inverse propensity weights can become unstable as `T` grows.[^sonabend-horizon]

DTRs also sharpen two points that are easy to understate in RL prose. First, treatment effects can be continuous. Kallus and Zhou show how off-policy evaluation changes when treatments are continuous: exact action matching fails, so the estimator must use treatment proximity through kernels and generalized propensity scores.[^kallus-continuous] Second, optimal DTR inference can be nonregular. Laber et al. emphasize that the max operator in Q-learning creates nonsmooth functionals, so uncertainty for an estimated optimal regime is not just an afterthought.[^laber-nonregular]

The chapter should use DTRs to teach the conceptual equivalence:

```text
DTR value estimation = OPE for a longitudinal treatment policy
```

The payoff is clarity. DTRs give the causal notation for histories, potential outcomes, consistency, positivity, and sequential ignorability. OPE gives the RL notation for policies, values, Q-functions, density ratios, and horizon-dependent variance. The mature view needs both.

### Identification Is Policy-Local

Another useful foundation is that OPE evidence is local to the evaluation policy. A dataset can identify the value of one target policy and say little about another. If a target policy stays close to the behavior policy, it may have good overlap, stable weights, and plausible Q-function extrapolation. A different target policy, evaluated on the same logs, may be nearly unsupported.

This is why policy class size matters. Evaluating one fixed policy is easier than selecting the best policy from a large class using the same OPE estimator. Once the analyst searches over many candidate policies, estimation error becomes a source of optimization bias. The chosen policy may be the one whose value was most overestimated. The Uehara review flags this issue in its discussion of offline policy optimization: the nuisance functions become policy-dependent, and out-of-distribution actions can lead to overestimation.[^uehara-policy-optimization]

For chapter 10b, the clean message is: single-policy OPE is the foundation; policy learning is a second layer. The chapter can explain the single-policy estimand first, then briefly note that optimizing over policies adds multiple-testing, overfitting, and distribution-shift problems.

## 3. Support, Propensities, And Overlap

Support is the first empirical question after identification. If the target policy asks for actions, treatment intensities, or state-action regions that the logs do not cover, the rest of the analysis becomes extrapolation. The Uehara review states the one-step positivity condition as support inclusion of the evaluation policy within the behavior policy.[^uehara-assumptions] In MDPs the analogous condition is stronger: the target policy's discounted state-action visitation distribution must be supported by the logged state-action distribution.[^uehara-mdp-support]

That is why propensities are not bookkeeping. They say how the data were generated. Importance sampling uses them directly. Doubly robust estimators use them to correct a value model. Even direct and FQE-style methods need the support story, because their model predictions under the evaluation policy are only as credible as the data regions that anchor them.

Overlap is not binary in useful analyses. A policy may be technically supported but practically unsupported because the probability ratios have long tails. Thomas, Theocharous, and Ghavamzadeh show the failure mode: if an evaluation policy takes an action that a behavior policy would never take in a state, ordinary importance sampling cannot evaluate that outcome; even when support holds, rare high-ratio events can create long-tailed weighted returns.[^thomas-support]

Continuous actions make this point impossible to avoid. Kallus and Zhou show that exact matching or rejection sampling breaks in continuous treatment spaces, because the target treatment will almost surely not equal the logged treatment. Their solution smooths across nearby treatments with kernels and generalized propensity scores, which makes bandwidth a real bias-variance choice rather than a tuning detail.[^kallus-continuous-bandwidth] Uehara, Shi, and Kallus add the sharper semiparametric warning: deterministic policies in continuous action spaces may not admit root-n value estimation without smoothing assumptions, and kernel methods can suffer in high-dimensional action spaces.[^uehara-deterministic]

The right chapter move is to show overlap before value. A credible OPE analysis should report:

- Action-support violations for the target policy.
- Propensity-ratio tails by time step.
- Cumulative-weight tails by horizon.
- Effective sample size for each candidate policy.
- State-occupancy overlap between logged and target-policy distributions, when estimable.
- Fraction of continuous or structured target actions outside high-density logged regions.
- Sensitivity of the value estimate to clipping, truncation, or support thresholds.

This section should come before the estimator taxonomy. If the support story is weak, the value estimate should be framed as extrapolation no matter how sophisticated the estimator is.

## 4. Why Horizon Changes Everything

The one-step case already has the central OPE tension: direct methods can be biased if the reward model is wrong, while importance weighting can be high variance if propensities are small. Sequential decisions add a second problem: ratios multiply.

Under ordinary trajectory importance sampling, a logged trajectory receives weight

```text
prod_t pi_e(A_t | S_t) / pi_b(A_t | S_t).
```

If the evaluation and behavior policies differ even modestly at each step, the product can become extremely variable. Thomas, Theocharous, and Ghavamzadeh show why confidence bounds become hard: importance-weighted returns can have long upper tails and enormous ranges.[^thomas-hcope-tail] Xie, Ma, and Wang state the horizon problem directly: standard IS methods can have variance that grows exponentially with the horizon.[^xie-horizon]

The key theoretical lesson in the Uehara review is sharper. In general NMDPs, exponential dependence on the horizon is unavoidable. In Markovian settings, extra structure can replace full trajectory ratios with marginal state or state-action density ratios, allowing polynomial horizon dependence under suitable conditions.[^uehara-horizon]

This is one of the most important ideas for the chapter:

> OPE is not hard merely because the action space is large. It is hard because the evaluation policy changes the distribution of future states.

That sentence is the bridge between causal inference and reinforcement learning. In a static causal problem, reweighting changes the distribution of treatment. In a sequential problem, reweighting also has to account for the distribution of histories or states created by earlier actions.

## 5. The Estimator Families

The field can be organized by which nuisance object the estimator trusts. That is more useful than listing algorithm names because each family answers a different practical question: do we trust a value model, a behavior-policy model, both, or an occupancy-ratio model?

### Value-Model Estimators: Direct Method And FQE

The direct method estimates a model of value and plugs the evaluation policy into it. In a contextual bandit, this means estimating `q(s,a) = E[R | S=s, A=a]` and averaging `q(s, pi_e)` over states. In sequential settings, the direct method estimates Q-functions or transition and reward models, then computes policy value from those estimates.

Fitted-Q evaluation (FQE) is the usual model-free direct method in RL. It recursively estimates the evaluation policy's Q-function using Bellman equations. The Uehara review describes FQE as a value-iteration-style method that can use flexible function approximation.[^uehara-fqe] Model-based OPE instead estimates transition and reward densities and plugs them into the g-formula, the causal-inference name for simulating the counterfactual distribution from the fitted structural model.[^uehara-model-based]

Direct methods are stable when the value model is good and the evaluation policy stays in well-covered regions. Their weakness is untestable approximation bias. Jiang and Li describe this clearly: regression-based OPE can have low variance, but in complex problems its approximation error may be hard to quantify.[^jiang-bias-variance] Farajtabar et al. add a practical version of the same warning: if the model is trained without regard to the evaluation policy, it may learn parts of the state-action space that do not matter for the evaluation and miss the parts that do.[^farajtabar-dm]

### Propensity Estimators: Importance Sampling

Importance sampling trusts the behavior policy probabilities. It does not require a reward or transition model. It corrects for the mismatch between the behavior and evaluation policies by reweighting observed rewards.

Its attraction is that, under support and known propensities, it can be unbiased. Its weakness is variance. The more the evaluation policy differs from the behavior policy, and the longer the horizon, the more the estimate is controlled by a small number of heavily weighted trajectories.

Per-decision or stepwise IS reduces some variance by weighting each reward only by ratios up to that time step. Weighted or self-normalized IS trades bias for lower variance by normalizing weights. These variants are often more usable, but their stability must not be confused with identification. If the effective sample size is tiny, the estimate remains weak evidence.

### Hybrid Estimators: Doubly Robust Methods

Doubly robust estimators combine the two preceding ideas. They use a model-based value estimate and add an importance-weighted correction for the model residual. In the clean setting, the estimator can be consistent if either the model side or the weighting side is correct.

Jiang and Li extend the doubly robust idea from contextual bandits to sequential decision-making and present it as a way to combine the low variance of regression with the bias protection of importance sampling.[^jiang-dr] Thomas and Brunskill then develop weighted doubly robust estimators and MAGIC, which blends model-based and importance-based estimates to reduce mean squared error.[^thomas-magic]

Farajtabar et al. focus on a subtle point that matters for a causal chapter: the model inside DR should not be treated as an ordinary prediction model. Their MRDR estimator learns the model component by minimizing the variance of the DR estimator.[^farajtabar-mrdr] That is a useful lesson for applied readers. In OPE, nuisance functions are not trained merely because they predict well. They are trained because they make the target estimand accurate.

### Occupancy-Ratio Estimators: Marginal Density Ratios

Marginal methods replace full trajectory ratios with state or state-action occupancy ratios. Instead of asking "how likely was this whole trajectory under the evaluation policy?", they ask "how much more likely is this state-action pair under the evaluation policy's induced distribution?"

This is the main way the Markov assumption pays for itself. If the observed state is sufficient, then histories that land in the same state can be pooled. Xie, Ma, and Wang's marginalized IS estimator recursively estimates target-policy state marginals at each step and obtains polynomial rather than exponential dependence on horizon in their setting.[^xie-mis] Uehara, Shi, and Kallus present marginal density ratios and Q-functions as the key objects for efficient long-horizon OPE.[^uehara-estimators]

The marginal-ratio view is also the best way to explain why OPE is a distribution-shift problem. Evaluating a policy means evaluating the state-action distribution that the policy induces. If that distribution is far from the logging distribution, then even a beautiful estimator becomes fragile.

## 6. Sensitivity Analysis, Robustness, And Bounds

Sensitivity analysis should be a prominent part of the chapter. OPE has many estimators, but the estimators matter less than the assumptions under which the estimand is identified and estimable. A strong OPE section should repeatedly ask:

> Which perturbation makes the policy conclusion change?

This framing keeps uncertainty from becoming a ceremonial interval after a point estimate. It turns the analysis into a stress test of the conclusion.

### Sensitivity To Unmeasured Confounding

The clean identification story assumes sequential ignorability. In observational data, this can fail. Uehara, Shi, and Kallus state the consequence plainly: if sequential ignorability is violated, the policy value is not identified without additional assumptions.[^uehara-confounding]

The review identifies three research directions. First, partial identification bounds use a sensitivity model. Instead of returning a single value, they return a range of values compatible with a bounded amount of hidden confounding. This is closest to classical causal sensitivity analysis. Kallus and Zhou give the infinite-horizon version: under unobserved confounding, exact policy value may be unidentifiable, so the target becomes sharp bounds under a sensitivity model.[^kallus-confounding-bounds]

Second, confounded MDP approaches rely on proxy variables. If a hidden confounder is not observed directly, observed proxy variables may still carry enough information to identify or bound the value under additional structure.

Third, partially observable MDP approaches treat hidden confounding as latent state. Negative-control ideas can sometimes recover identification in special settings, but the required assumptions can be hard to validate.

The practical chapter message should be restrained:

> When hidden confounding is plausible, OPE should report bounds or sensitivity curves, not a single definitive value.

A useful sensitivity curve would vary the allowed degree of confounding and show when the policy ranking changes. If a conclusion survives only under zero hidden confounding, it is not robust evidence.

### Sensitivity To Overlap And Clipping

Overlap diagnostics should already have been reported before value. The sensitivity question is more specific: how much of the conclusion depends on rare high-weight observations?

This is where the high-confidence OPE paper is useful. It shows that the upper tail of importance-weighted returns can dominate the range of the estimator and weaken confidence bounds.[^thomas-hcope-tail] In chapter prose, the lesson is not just "use clipping." The lesson is to report how much the estimate and interval move as the analyst changes clipping thresholds, support filters, or effective-sample-size cutoffs.

Support sensitivity should be reported before policy value. If the support story is weak, the value estimate should be framed as extrapolation.

### Sensitivity To Nuisance Estimation

Modern OPE uses nuisance functions: behavior policies, Q-functions, density ratios, transition models, reward models. The Uehara review emphasizes that efficient estimators depend on rates for nuisance functions and often use sample splitting to avoid overfitting.[^uehara-efficiency] It also notes that FQE and minimax Q-learning require realizability, completeness, or related assumptions, and that hyperparameter selection is difficult because Q-function estimation lacks a natural cross-validation criterion.[^uehara-fqe]

The sensitivity question is therefore:

> Does the value estimate survive reasonable changes in nuisance models?

The chapter should recommend triangulation: direct, IS, DR, and marginal-ratio estimates should be compared when feasible. Disagreement is not an embarrassment. It is diagnostic information about which assumptions carry the conclusion.

### Sensitivity To Distribution Shift

Distribution shift is a different problem from unmeasured confounding. Here the policy value may be identified for the logged environment, but the future environment differs. Demand changes, user mix changes, prices change, constraints change, or the state-transition kernel drifts.

Wang, Gao, and Zha motivate robust OPE exactly this way: the environment where the target policy is deployed may deviate from the past environment where logs were collected.[^wang-shift] Their framework constructs robust and optimistic estimates that serve as lower and upper confidence bounds under suitable uncertainty sets.[^wang-robust]

The chapter can express this without heavy optimization detail. A robust OPE analysis asks:

> If the estimated transition or visitation distribution is perturbed within a plausible uncertainty set, how bad can the policy value be?

That question turns OPE into a stability analysis. If the policy wins only under the empirical distribution but loses under small plausible shifts, the point estimate is fragile. If it stays above the incumbent under a range of perturbations, the evidence is stronger.

### High-Confidence Lower Bounds

The point estimate is not enough. High-confidence OPE asks for a lower confidence bound on policy value. Thomas, Theocharous, and Ghavamzadeh formulate this directly: use logged trajectories to compute a lower bound on the expected return of an evaluation policy.[^thomas-hcope] Their key challenge is the heavy-tailed behavior of importance-weighted returns, which makes naive concentration bounds loose.

The right interpretation is conservative. A lower bound asks what can be said under sampling uncertainty. A robust bound asks what can be said when the transition or visitation distribution is perturbed. A confounding-sensitivity bound asks what can be said when sequential ignorability is weakened. These are not three unrelated technical tricks. They are three versions of the same epistemic discipline: do not let a point estimate pretend to be stronger evidence than it is.

## 7. A Minimal OPE Diagnostic Checklist

For chapter 10b, the goal is not to prescribe a production workflow. It is to teach what a credible OPE analysis must show.

1. State the estimand.

Name the evaluation policy, behavior policy, horizon, discounting, reward, and unit of analysis. If the policy is deterministic in a continuous action space, note that standard root-n OPE may fail without smoothing assumptions.[^uehara-deterministic]

2. State the identification assumptions.

List support, consistency, sequential ignorability, and the structural time assumption: contextual bandit, NMDP, TMDP, MDP, POMDP, or general causal graph.

3. Show overlap before value.

Report action support, propensity-ratio tails, cumulative-weight tails, effective sample size, state-occupancy overlap, and continuous-action proximity when relevant.

4. Use estimator triangulation.

Compare at least one model-based or direct estimate, one weighting estimate when feasible, and one doubly robust or marginal-ratio estimate when the setting supports it.

5. Put uncertainty around the estimate.

Report intervals or lower bounds, and explain whether they are sampling intervals, high-confidence lower bounds, robust bounds, or sensitivity bounds.

6. Run sensitivity analysis.

Vary hidden-confounding strength, support thresholds, clipping levels, nuisance models, and distribution-shift radii. The conclusion should say which perturbations change the policy ranking or sign of the value difference.

7. Separate evaluation from optimization.

If the same OPE estimator is used to select the best policy from a large class, the analysis has become offline policy optimization. That introduces overfitting and out-of-distribution action problems beyond single-policy evaluation.[^uehara-policy-optimization]

## 8. How This Supports The Later Applications Chapter

The later chapter can refer back to this memo for the theory and diagnostics. It should not have to re-explain why full-trajectory IS suffers from horizon, why FQE can extrapolate, why DR uses both a model and propensities, why continuous actions require smoothing, or why robust bounds are a form of sensitivity analysis.

The division of labor should be:

- Chapter 10b: causal estimands, identification, DTR bridge, support diagnostics, estimator families, efficiency, uncertainty, sensitivity analysis.
- Later applications chapter: concrete systems, case evidence, operational constraints, experiment designs, guardrails, and monitoring.

That separation keeps the foundations clean. It also lets later examples use OPE terms precisely instead of reintroducing them ad hoc.

The deeper practice search sources are now landed locally. They remain backend-only. The strongest practice evidence is for tooling, recommender evaluation, randomized-holdout triage, pricing-policy valuation, and estimator-robustness workflows. The evidence is thinner for full sensitivity-analysis practice outside the academic papers, so the main chapter should lean on Saito et al., Wang et al., Kallus and Zhou, Thomas et al., and Sakhi et al. for robustness and sensitivity claims. The Sony and Criteo practice pages are useful only as proxy local text because direct downloads were blocked in this environment. Their associated AAAI and NeurIPS papers are cleanly sourced.
